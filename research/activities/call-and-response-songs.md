# Call and Response Songs

## Identity and context

- **Description:** A solo leader sings a phrase and a group sings an answer; participants take turns leading.
- **Group and mode:** Carnegie Hall documents this for kindergarten singing together, in person and live; no fixed size is given.
- **Co-presence and timing:** The group must hear a call before answering and coordinate its sung response. The source activity is in person; it does not document a remote version or a numerical latency tolerance.
- **Facilitator:** A teacher chooses the songs, teaches the response, and cues turns.
- **Experience:** Group performance through listening and responding.

## Preparation

Choose a call-and-response song and explain which phrase belongs to the soloist and which to the group. No digital materials are needed.

## Procedure

1. Warm up with a simple call-and-response song.
2. Explain that the leader sings a phrase and the group sings it back in an echo song.
3. Sing the song with the teacher leading and group responding.
4. Repeat while students take turns as soloist or leader.
5. Optionally add movements that distinguish soloist and group turns, then introduce a song whose response differs from the call.

## Rules and choices

- **Roles:** One soloist/leader and the responding group; leadership rotates.
- **Trigger:** The end of each call cues a group response. Song structure governs repetition and ending.
- **Visibility:** Sound is shared; leader/group movements can add visual cues.
- **Completion:** A song or host-selected set of turns ends. The source does not require everyone to lead.

## Variants

The [Carnegie Hall guide](https://www.carnegiehall.org/Education/Programs/Music-Educators-Toolbox/K-Call-and-Response-Songs) contrasts an echoed phrase with a song having a different response and suggests movements indicating whose turn it is.

## Exceptions and open questions

The guide does not prescribe leader-selection order, a response to a missed cue, or a rule for a child declining the solo role.

## Access and participation

The activity relies on hearing and vocal response. The documented movement cues add another way to perceive whose turn it is but are not presented as a full accessibility solution. Alternatives for a participant who cannot hear, sing, or lead are not specified and require group-specific planning. A remote group singing the response together would also need audio delay low and stable enough for ensemble timing. Research on networked music performance commonly discusses an end-to-end delay below roughly 25 milliseconds; ordinary conferencing is not a reliable substitute for the in-person form. This is a transport feasibility constraint, not a rule in Carnegie Hall's activity. Specialized low-latency systems can support some remote music making under favorable conditions; [JackTrip's guidance](https://support.jacktrip.com/how-to-optimize-latency-when-using-jacktrip) stresses that connection, distance, hardware, and jitter all matter.

## Mechanisms and possible software role

**Mechanisms:** Alternating role-specific turns; repeatable call/response unit; optional leader rotation and parallel movement cue. Software might show lyrics or cue a leader, but it cannot determine whether people sang together without additional observation. No stored submission is part of the source activity. A remote host must declare and satisfy live-audio timing requirements before claiming to support group singing; playing a recording or collecting separate voices would be a different variant.
