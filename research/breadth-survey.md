# Pass 2: breadth survey of guided group activities

**Status:** Pass 2 gap sweep complete for the named targets; still a contrast sample, not an exhaustive inventory.

**Question:** Which kinds of simple group activities should challenge a portable activity format?

This map samples runnable activities rather than occasions. A birthday party, dinner, school day, or workshop may contain several of them. Each linked source describes the named activity or its rules. The short mechanism labels below are analysis for comparing activities; they are not claims that the sources use those terms.

## Rituals and group care

| Activity or structure | Minimal pattern | Distinguishing mechanism | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morning Meeting | A class gathers for greeting, sharing, a group activity, and a teacher's message. | Ordered **session of components**, one of which is another activity | [Responsive Classroom](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org/what-is-morning-meeting/) |

| Gratitude Circle | Each participant names something they are grateful for and why. | Circular personal sharing with a repeated prompt | [Greater Good Science Center](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/gratitude_activities_for_the_classroom) |
| Gratitude Spies | Each child secretly draws a peer's name, observes that person during the day, then shares appreciation in an end-of-day circle. | Private assignment, delayed observation, later public acknowledgement | [Greater Good Science Center](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/gratitude_activities_for_the_classroom) |

Morning Meeting and CASEL's three practices are **composition patterns**, not one atomic game. They show why a portable format may need a session plan that sequences activities, prompts, and transitions. These sources describe educational practices; this survey does not treat them as evidence of measured benefits. Personal sharing also raises a design question about whether someone may pass, substitute a prompt, or keep a contribution private; the cited brief instructions do not settle those rules.

These are descriptions of particular documented variants, not claims about all play in those countries. The Ampe thesis gives both ten and eleven points as a winning threshold; that rule requires another source or an explicit local choice before a runnable card claims one value. A future card should retain each game's name and context alongside any mechanism tags. Extracting a general mechanic does not establish permission to strip cultural context from an activity package.

## Accessibility as a cross-cutting dimension

[RNIB's party guide](https://www.rnib.org.uk/documents/465/APDF-RE190403_Parties_and_Playdates-v07.pdf) asks hosts to explain rules and stages, announce starts and finishes, and use specific spatial language for children with vision impairment. It names Pass the Parcel as a game that uses sound and touch and can allow peer help with unwrapping. [Playworks' access guidance](https://www.playworks.org/resource/tips-for-making-play-access-easier/) says to ask participants and caregivers what support works and to adapt for the individual. These are facilitation requirements across many activities, not one additional game category.

For each full card, record the sensory, movement, language, and disclosure demands that the documented rules create; source-backed adaptations; and any accommodations that still require participant or host choice. Do not infer that a blindfold mechanic is automatically accessible to every participant. For software, prompts and signals may need more than one presentation mode, while physical help and consent remain human decisions.

## Early patterns from this sample

- **The same mechanism crosses settings.** Pass the Parcel, Wonderball, and Roll the Orange all use a physical token, but the token grants different rights: opening, performing a task, or speaking.
- **A host can be central even when software does little.** Musical Statues, Pass the Parcel, and The Maze Game rely on a person to signal, judge, or reveal information.
- **Simple activities can combine.** A dinner may put Roll the Orange around Rose & Thorn; an occasion or session therefore needs a sequence of activities, not merely one activity instance. This is an inference from their documented rules, not a claim that the cited sources prescribe that combination.
- **Privacy and visibility vary.** Gratefulness Grab Bag mixes private slips; Exquisite Corpse hides previous contributions; BeReal gates viewing on posting; critique controls when opinions may be offered.
- **Not every group activity has a stored artifact.** Some produce a conversation, shared experience, or physical result. A portable format cannot require a digital submission in every activity.
- **The mechanism may depend on a particular medium.** Call and Response uses audible alternation; Fukuwarai uses limited vision and spoken guidance; cooking uses a real recipe and ingredients. A host can provide digital coordination without replacing the participant action.
- **Remote timing can limit portability.** The [Call and Response card](activities/call-and-response-songs.md) is sourced as an in-person activity. A generic internet call does not guarantee the low, stable audio delay needed for coordinated group singing; a remote variant must state its actual medium and timing conditions.
- **Composition needs its own layer.** Morning Meeting and the SEL 3 practices define an order around activities. Their sequence belongs to a session or occasion plan, while the embedded activity still needs its own rules.

## Coverage limits and Pass 3 selection

This remains a deliberate contrast sample, not a count of all activity types or evidence that these activities are popular or effective. The named gap sweep adds music, drama, group care, cooking, access, and sources from a Brazilian federal institute, a Ghanaian university, and a Japanese embassy. It still draws heavily on English-language US and UK guides and does not represent the range of any country or tradition. Longer asynchronous exchanges and participant-led care groups remain thinly sampled.

Source types vary: Playworks and the Family Dinner Project publish runnable instructions; the BeReal Help Center documents current product behavior; the National Gallery's One Sky description is an account of a particular project rather than a standardized game. None of these sources alone establishes outcomes or adoption.
